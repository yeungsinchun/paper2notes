27.1

Nuclear fission and fusion

A heavy nucleus splits, light nuclei join — both release energy because the products weigh less than the reactants. The released neutrons can trigger more fission, which is how a chain reaction is sustained in a reactor but loses control in a bomb, and why fusion needs stellar temperatures.

Learning objectives

Students should be able to:

  • realise the release of energy in nuclear fission and fusion

  • realise nuclear chain reaction

  • realise nuclear fusion as the source of solar energy

ANuclear fission: a heavy nucleus splits

Definition

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into lighter nuclei. When a heavy nucleus is bombarded by a neutron, it may split and release energy.

\[ {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} + \text{energy} \]
  • The reaction can only be triggered by a slow neutron. A fast neutron passes through the U-235 nucleus without being captured.
  • The nuclei formed are called fission fragments. One U-235 nucleus can split in several different ways, so the products vary; the number of neutrons is usually 2 or 3:
\[ {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \rightarrow {}^{144}_{54}\mathrm{Xe} + {}^{90}_{38}\mathrm{Sr} + 2\,{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} + \text{energy} \]
\[ {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \rightarrow {}^{148}_{57}\mathrm{La} + {}^{85}_{35}\mathrm{Br} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} + \text{energy} \]
  • Natural uranium is ~0.7% U-235 and ~99.3% U-238; only the U-235 fraction undergoes this fission readily.
  • Each fission releases about \(2.8 \times 10^{-11}\) J. The amount sounds tiny, but a gram of U-235 contains \( \sim 10^{21}\) nuclei.

BChain reaction and critical mass

Definition

A chain reaction occurs when the neutrons emitted in one fission go on to trigger fission in nearby nuclei, creating a self-sustaining sequence.

  • If each fission triggers, on average, less than one further fission, the chain dies out (subcritical). Neutrons escape the sample before they find another U-235 nucleus.
  • If it triggers exactly one, the rate stays steady (critical). This is the condition in a power reactor at constant output.
  • If it triggers more than one, the rate grows rapidly (supercritical) — the condition in a bomb.
  • Whether the chain sustains depends on the mass, density and purity of the fission material. The minimum mass needed for a steady chain is the critical mass. Below it, too many neutrons escape.
Domino analogy

Dominoes in a pyramid fall like a chain, but spacing matters: pack them too far apart and the fall stops. U-235 must be sufficiently concentrated so that a neutron meets another nucleus before it leaves the lump.

Concept check

Which of the following must be true for a fission chain reaction to sustain?

C. One-for-one is the threshold; more gives a growing chain, fewer and it dies. A is false (2–3 neutrons is the norm); B reverses the slow-neutron requirement; D is natural uranium, far below the enrichment needed for a sustained chain.

CFission bomb versus fission reactor

A chain reaction that loses control releases its energy in a tiny fraction of a second — a fission bomb (or atomic bomb). A chain reaction that is controlled releases it steadily — a reactor.

Fission bombFission reactor (power station)
Enrichment of U-235>90% (highly enriched)~3% (low enriched)
ChainSupercritical; rate grows uncheckedCritical; rate held steady
Energy releaseExplosive, in <1 sSteady heat over months
ResultBlast and radiationHot water → steam → turbines → electricity

Natural uranium (~0.7% U-235) must be enriched before use in either device — slightly for a reactor, greatly for a bomb. This is why a reactor can never explode like a bomb.

ComponentJob
Fuel rodsContain the uranium fuel (~3% U-235) where fission occurs
Control rodsAbsorb neutrons. Pushed in → fewer neutrons cause fission → chain slows; pulled out → faster
Pressurized water circuit / moderatorCarries heat from vessel to boiler; pressurized water also slows neutrons to the low speeds U-235 needs

Fission vs α-decay — Example 27.1

Both split a nucleus, but fission is triggered by a neutron and its rate can be controlled (rods in/out); α-decay is spontaneous with a fixed half-life and cannot be hastened or slowed. Fission gives a variety of fragments; α-decay of a given nuclide always gives the same daughter and α particle.

Nuclear fissionα-decay
Can it be triggered?Yes, by neutron bombardmentNo — spontaneous
Can the rate be controlled?Yes (control rods, concentration)No — set by half-life
Products?Varies (Ba+Kr, Xe+Sr, La+Br…)Fixed for the nuclide

Concept check

Which statement about a fission reactor is correct?

C is the textbook description. A is the bomb enrichment; B reverses rods in/out; D confuses a meltdown (overheating fuel, as at Fukushima where the chain had already stopped, but decay heat remained) with a nuclear explosion — the enrichment is far too low.

DNuclear fusion: light nuclei join

Definition

Nuclear fusion is the joining of light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus. Energy is released because the product weighs slightly less than the reactants.

\[ {}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} + \text{energy} \]
  • The electrostatic repulsion between the two positively charged nuclei makes fusion difficult to initiate. The nuclei must be given enough kinetic energy to overcome it — that means a temperature of about \(10^{7}\) K.
  • The D–T reaction above releases about \(2.8 \times 10^{-12}\) J per reaction (\(17.6\) MeV). Like fission, an enormous number of reactions per gram makes the total large.
  • On Earth the D–T reaction is achieved briefly in a fusion (hydrogen) bomb: a small fission bomb provides the extreme temperature to ignite fusion. The fusion itself is uncontrollable and about 1000 times more destructive than the fission trigger.

Concept check

(a) Energy is released only when heavy nuclei split, not when light nuclei fuse.

False. Both directions release energy because both move toward more tightly bound nuclei (near iron-56) — splitting heavy nuclei and joining light ones both lose mass. See the binding-energy curve in 27.2.

(b) Light nuclei fuse only when bombarded by neutrons.

False. Fusion is between two light nuclei; the barrier is Coulomb repulsion, not a neutron trigger. High temperature (kinetic energy) is what is needed, not an incoming neutron.

(c) The temperature must be very high for fusion to occur.

True. About \(10^{7}\) K is needed on Earth; the Sun's core is of this order.

EThe Sun, the hydrogen bomb and the fusion reactor

The Sun is a natural fusion reactor. Deep in its core, at about \(1.5 \times 10^{7}\) K, millions of tonnes of light nuclei fuse every second. The energy appears first as kinetic energy and gamma photons, then works its way out as the sunlight and warmth we feel. About 74% of the Sun's mass is hydrogen and 25% helium; over billions of years the hydrogen fraction slowly falls and the helium fraction rises as fusion proceeds.

  • Why fusion hardly occurs on Earth at ordinary temperatures: nuclei at room temperature have far too little kinetic energy to overcome the Coulomb repulsion. Only in the extreme heat of a star, or briefly in an explosion, do collisions succeed.
  • Hydrogen bomb: the heat to ignite D–T fusion is supplied by a small fission primary. Once ignited, the secondary fusion fuel (often lithium deuteride) fuses uncontrollably. Energy per kilogram of D+T fuel is about 5 times that per kilogram of U-235 — hence a single fusion stage is vastly more destructive.

Fusion reactor — why it is hard

A practical fusion power plant would be attractive: fuel (deuterium from seawater) is cheap and almost unlimited, the waste product helium is non-toxic and non-radioactive, and there is no long-lived fission waste. Two challenges have prevented a commercial plant so far:

  • Heating the fuel under control to fusion temperature and keeping it hot long enough for the reaction to give net energy.
  • Containing the fuel: no ordinary container can hold a gas at \(10^{7}\) K. Experimental reactors use magnetic fields to confine the hot plasma without touching a wall (Fig. 27.10).
  • As yet, no commercial fusion power plant exists. Research devices heat the fuel and confine it magnetically, but have not yet run as a steady power source.
History — discovery of fission

Enrico Fermi bombarded uranium with neutrons in 1934 and thought he had made an element heavier than uranium. Chemist Otto Hahn and physicist Lise Meitner repeated the work; in 1938 Hahn showed the products were actually lighter elements, and Meitner correctly interpreted the reaction as fission. Hahn received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone; many believe Meitner should have shared it.

Concept check

Which of the following generates energy by nuclear fusion?

B — the Sun's power is D–D and D–T fusion chains. A and C are fission; D is chemical burning.

Concept check

Which is not an advantage of fusion power over fission?

D is the distractor: sunlight is already fusion energy arriving; it cannot trigger fusion on Earth. Sunlight at the surface is ~300 K, far below \(10^{7}\) K. A–C are textbook advantages.

FNuclear power — for and against

In 2013 about 73% of France's electricity and about 20% of the world's was nuclear; France ran 58 reactors, the US 104, China 20 (Fig. 27.11). The textbook frames the debate as a set of testable facts versus values that society must weigh.

For nuclear powerAgainst nuclear power
Large-scale alternative as fossil fuels run out; nuclear fuel is generally cheaper per unit energyNuclear accidents have widespread, long-lasting impact (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011)
No air pollutants and no carbon dioxide from the reactor itselfRadioactive waste includes long-lived nuclides (e.g. I-129, half-life 15.7 million y); safe disposal is difficult, expensive and unresolved — spent fuel pools, then deep geological burial
Proven technology for steady base-load electricityHigher maintenance, safety and shutdown costs; risk that civil nuclear spread increases nuclear-weapons risk
  • For: “readily available, large-scale alternative”, “no air pollutants / CO2”, “cheaper fuel” are the textbook bullets.
  • Against: “radioactive waste with long half-lives — disposal is difficult and expensive”, “accidents may result in disasters”, “higher maintenance/safety/shutdown costs” and “risk of nuclear war” are the textbook bullets.
  • Spent fuels are cooled in water pools at the plant for 10–20 y before any reprocessing or burial (Fig. a/b p.103). In 2009 about 75% of US spent fuel was still in those pools — no permanent solution yet. Most long-term plans bury steel containers deep underground.
  • Exam attitude: the question may ask you to “state two disadvantages” or “compare costs and benefits with other methods”. Use the textbook list, not an invented one, and keep benefits and risks separate as the syllabus values-attitudes section intends.
Snapshots — Chernobyl & Fukushima (not examinable detail)

Chernobyl (1986, USSR): chain reaction got out of control, vessel exploded, radioactive material spread >1000 km (alarm in Sweden). Deaths from 4000 to 93 000 estimated; damaged reactor now under a concrete coffin.

Fukushima (2011, Japan): chain reaction had already stopped after the earthquake, but decay heat from the fuel overheated the reactors when cooling and pools were damaged by the tsunami. Three reactors melted down, food contaminated 320 km away, 20 km forbidden zone, cleanup still decades long. A plant at ~3% enrichment cannot explode like a bomb — the enrichment is far too low.

Concept check

Which of the following is/are disadvantages of fission power listed in the textbook?

  1. It must be heated to \(10^{7}\) K before fission can start.
  2. Its waste contains nuclides with extremely long half-lives.
  3. Its fuel can be easily obtained from seawater.

(2) is the textbook “against”. (1) is a challenge for fusion, not fission — fission needs slow neutrons, not extreme heat. (3) is an advantage of fusion fuel. Answer B.
