26.1

Rate of radioactive decay

Decay is random for one nucleus and perfectly predictable for a large sample. This section builds the two working tools, half-life and activity, shows how to read them from real (background-contaminated) data, and then, as extension, the decay constant and the exponential law that let you solve for any time, not only whole half-lives.

Learning objectives

Students should be able to:

  • examine the random nature of radioactive decay

  • state the proportional relationship between the activity of a sample and the number of undecayed nuclei

  • define half-life as the period of time over which the number of radioactive nuclei decreases by a factor of one-half

  • determine the half-life of a radioisotope from its decay graph or from numerical data

  • realise the existence of background radiation

  • solve problems involving radioactive decay

  • represent the number of undecayed nuclei by the exponential law of decay \( N = N_0 e^{-kt} \) extension

  • apply the exponential law of decay \( N = N_0 e^{-kt} \) to solve problems extension

  • relate the decay constant and the half-life extension

ADecay is random, but a sample is predictable

Random and spontaneous

Radioactive decay is random: it is impossible to predict when a particular nucleus will decay, and every undecayed nucleus of a given nuclide has the same chance of decaying in the next second, however old it is. It is spontaneous: it happens on its own and nothing external (temperature, pressure, chemical bonding, an electric field) speeds it up or slows it down.

  • Because every nucleus has the same chance per unit time, the number decaying per second is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present. Fewer survivors, fewer decays. That is why the rate falls as the sample decays.
  • With a large number of nuclei (a microgram of anything contains about \( 10^{15} \) atoms) the statistical average is so reliable that the decay of a sample follows a smooth, predictable curve.
  • With very few nuclei the average is not reliable. If a million boxes each hold two nuclei and you wait one half-life, some boxes will have 0 left, some 1, some 2; only the average is 1. "Half of two nuclei decays after one half-life" is not a meaningful statement.
  • Evidence of randomness: repeated equal-time counts of the same source (or of background) are never identical, and cloud-chamber tracks appear at random places and moments.

Concept check

A sample contains a very large number of nuclei of one radionuclide. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. The nuclei that have survived longest are the most likely to decay next.
  2. The number decaying per second falls as time goes on.
  3. Cooling the sample would slow its decay.

(1) is false: nuclei do not age; every survivor has the same chance per second. (2) is true: fewer undecayed nuclei remain, so fewer decay per second. (3) is false: decay is spontaneous and unaffected by temperature. Answer C.

BHalf-life

Definition

The half-life \( T_{1/2} \) of a radionuclide is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei in a sample to fall to half its initial value. Equivalently, it is the time for the activity to fall to half. After \( n \) half-lives,

\[ N = N_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}, \qquad n = \frac{t}{T_{1/2}} \]
  • Half-life is a property of the nuclide, not of the sample. A gram and a microgram of iodine-131 both have \( T_{1/2} = 8 \) d. Heating, cooling, dissolving or splitting the sample does not change it.
  • Half-life is not "how long the source lasts". After one half-life half the nuclei are still undecayed; after two, a quarter; the number approaches zero but a sample is never "finished" at any particular moment.
  • The decayed nuclei do not disappear. They become the daughter nuclide, so the total number of nuclei (and, very nearly, the mass) stays constant while the sample becomes progressively more daughter and less parent.
  • Half-lives range over more than twenty powers of ten:

Worked example

A sample of a radionuclide with half-life 3 days has mass 100 g. (a) What mass of the nuclide remains after 12 days? (b) What mass has decayed? (c) What was the mass 6 days before the start?

  1. (a) \( n = 12/3 = 4 \) half-lives. Remaining \( = 100 \times (1/2)^4 = 6.25 \) g.
  2. (b) Decayed \( = 100 - 6.25 = 93.75 \) g. (It is still in the sample, as daughter nuclide.)
  3. (c) Going backwards two half-lives doubles twice: \( 100 \times 2^2 = 400 \) g.

Common slips: giving 6.25 g for "decayed", or 75 g (two half-lives) for "decayed in 12 days".

Concept check

Which of the following statements about the half-life of a radioactive source is/are correct?

  1. It is the time for the activity of the source to fall to half its value.
  2. It is the time the source remains radioactive.
  3. It is the time for the number of undecayed nuclei to fall to half its value.

(3) is the definition and (1) follows because \( A \propto N \). (2) is the standard misconception: after one half-life the source is still radioactive with half its nuclei left. Answer B.

Concept check

A source contains only radioactive nuclide X, which decays to stable Y. After one half-life of X, which of the following is/are correct?

  1. The number of X nuclei is about half the initial number.
  2. The number of Y nuclei is about twice its initial value.
  3. The activity of the source is about half its initial value.

(1) and (3) are the definition of half-life applied to \( N \) and \( A \). (2) is a trap: the source started with no Y, so there is no "initial value" to double. After one half-life the Y nuclei equal the X nuclei. Answer C.

Concept check

A pure sample of a radionuclide decays to a stable daughter. After 2 years the sample is 2% parent and 98% daughter by number. What was its composition after 1 year?

Whatever fraction survives one year, the same fraction of that survives the next year. So \( f^2 = 0.02 \), \( f = \sqrt{0.02} = 0.14 \): C. A assumes the fraction falls linearly; B assumes half of the loss happens each year; D doubles instead of taking the square root.

CActivity

Definition

The activity \( A \) of a sample is the number of nuclei that decay per unit time. Unit: the becquerel (Bq), 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. Because every undecayed nucleus has the same chance of decaying per second, activity is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei:

\[ A \propto N \qquad\text{so}\qquad A = A_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n} \]
  • Activity depends on two things: how many undecayed nuclei there are, and the nuclide's half-life (a short half-life means each nucleus is more likely to decay per second). It does not depend on the type of radiation emitted.
  • Halving a sample halves \( N \) and therefore \( A \). It leaves the half-life unchanged.
  • Count rate is not activity. A GM tube some distance away records far fewer counts per second than the activity in Bq: radiation is emitted in all directions and the tube intercepts only a small fraction; some is absorbed on the way; the tube does not register every particle that enters it. The corrected count rate is proportional to activity, so it halves on the same clock and can be used to find the half-life.
  • Non-integer numbers of half-lives are allowed: \( n = t / T_{1/2} \) need not be a whole number. \( A = A_0 (1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}} \) works for any \( t \).
Worked example

Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. A sample has activity 200 Bq after 30 days. What was its initial activity?

  1. \( n = 30/8 = 3.75 \) half-lives (do not round to 3 or 4).
  2. \( A_0 = A \times 2^{n} = 200 \times 2^{3.75} = 200 \times 13.45 = 2690 \) Bq.

Rounding \( n \) to 4 gives 3200 Bq; to 3 gives 1600 Bq; multiplying by 3.75 gives 750 Bq. All three appear as distractors.

Concept check

Half of a powdered radioactive sample is removed. For the half that remains, which of the following are halved compared with the original sample?

  1. the activity
  2. the half-life
  3. the number of undecayed nuclei

Half the powder means half the nuclei (3), and since \( A \propto N \) half the activity (1). The half-life is a property of the nuclide and is unchanged. Answer A.

Concept check

On which of the following does the activity of a radioactive source depend?

  1. the nature of the radiation emitted
  2. the half-life of the nuclide
  3. the number of undecayed nuclei

\( A = kN \): the number of nuclei (3) and, through \( k = \ln 2 / T_{1/2} \), the half-life (2). Whether the decay gives α, β or γ has no bearing on how many decays occur per second. Answer D.

Concept check

Two samples P and Q are of the same radionuclide. P has twice as many undecayed nuclei as Q. Which of the following is correct?

C: \( A \propto N \) gives the factor of two; half-life is fixed by the nuclide, so both halve on the same clock. A and D make the half-life depend on the amount; B ignores \( N \).

DReading half-life from real data

Corrected count rate

Background radiation (cosmic rays, rocks and soil, building materials, food and drink, radon in the air) gives a GM tube a small, roughly steady count rate even with no source nearby. The corrected count rate is the recorded count rate minus the background count rate, and it is the quantity proportional to the activity of the source.

\[ \text{corrected count rate} = \text{recorded count rate} - \text{background count rate} \]
Worked example

In Example 26.1 the recorded count rate starts at 840 min-1 and flattens at about 40 min-1. Find the half-life.

  1. Background \( \approx 40 \) min-1 (the level the graph flattens to). Corrected initial rate \( = 840 - 40 = 800 \) min-1.
  2. After one half-life the corrected rate is 400 min-1, so the recorded rate is \( 400 + 40 = 440 \) min-1.
  3. Read the time at which the recorded curve passes 440 min-1: 10 h. So \( T_{1/2} = 10 \) h.

Reading the time to fall from 840 to 420 on the uncorrected curve gives a half-life that is too long. Always halve the corrected value, then add background back before reading the graph.

  • Why the curve flattens. The corrected count rate approaches zero but never quite reaches it (each half-life only halves what is left). Long before that, the source's contribution has fallen below the background, so the recorded rate levels off at the background value.
  • From a table. Subtract background from every reading, then find the half-life from the corrected column (or plot it and read the graph). A single corrected reading can come out slightly negative or below background because both source and background counts fluctuate randomly; treat it as "about zero".
  • Each reading more than half the previous in equal time steps does not mean a long half-life; it is the signature of an uncorrected background. Subtract it and check again.
  • Scatter. Points that do not lie exactly on a smooth curve are expected: decay is random. Draw the best smooth curve through them.
  • A second run with a weaker source of the same nuclide starts lower but has the same half-life and flattens at the same background level.
  • Reduce background error by counting for longer and by measuring background for a long time before and after the experiment.
Worked example

A GM counter near a source reads 1000 min-1. Two half-lives later it reads 400 min-1. (a) Find the background count rate. (b) What will it read after a further half-life?

  1. (a) Let background be \( b \). Corrected start \( = 1000 - b \). After two half-lives corrected \( = (1000 - b)/4 \), so recorded \( = (1000 - b)/4 + b = 400 \). Then \( 1000 - b + 4b = 1600 \), \( b = 200 \) min-1.
  2. (b) Corrected after three half-lives \( = 800/8 = 100 \); recorded \( = 100 + 200 = 300 \) min-1.

Dividing 1000 by 4 and comparing with 400 "proves" nothing: the mismatch is the background.

Concept check

A GM counter records 140 min-1 when a source is first placed near it. After a long time the reading settles at 20 min-1. At what recorded count rate should you read the graph to find one half-life?

Corrected start \( = 140 - 20 = 120 \); half of that is 60; recorded \( = 60 + 20 = 80 \) min-1, B. A halves the uncorrected reading; C forgets to add the background back before reading the recorded curve; D halves the background.

Concept check

Counts per minute from a source are recorded every minute, with background about 100 min-1: 900, 417, 225, 150, 113, 92, 102. Which explanation of the reading of 92 is best?

By 5 minutes the corrected rate is close to zero, so the reading is essentially background, and background counts vary randomly from minute to minute: B. Activity cannot be negative (A); half-life is constant (C); a slightly low count is normal, not a fault (D). From the first three corrected values, 800, 317, 125, each minute multiplies by about 0.4, giving \( T_{1/2} \approx 45 \) s.

EComparing two sources

A longer half-life does not mean a weaker source, and a stronger source now does not mean a longer-lasting one. Both the starting activity and the half-life matter. Amy and Bob each buy a device that stops working when the source's activity falls below 1000 Bq:

Initial activityHalf-lifeActivity year by yearWorking life
Amy8000 Bq2 y8000 → 4000 (2 y) → 2000 (4 y) → 1000 (6 y)about 6 y
Bob16 000 Bq1 y16 000 → 8000 (1 y) → 4000 (2 y) → 2000 (3 y) → 1000 (4 y)about 4 y
  • Bob's source starts stronger but dies first: four halvings in 4 years against Amy's three in 6 years.
  • For equal numbers of nuclei, the shorter half-life gives the higher activity (each nucleus is more likely to decay per second). For unequal amounts you must compare \( A = kN \) directly.
  • On a graph of activity against time, the curve that falls more steeply as a fraction of its current value has the shorter half-life. Two curves that cross have equal activity at that moment, but the one with the shorter half-life has fewer undecayed nuclei there (\( N = A/k \) with larger \( k \)).

Concept check

Initially a sample contains 4 times as many nuclei of X (half-life \( t \)) as of Y (half-life \( 2t \)). After how long are the numbers of X and Y nuclei equal?

Set \( 4N(1/2)^{T/t} = N(1/2)^{T/2t} \). Writing 4 as \( 2^2 \): \( 2 - T/t = -T/2t \), so \( T/2t = 2 \), \( T = 4t \): D. Check: after \( 4t \), X has had 4 half-lives (factor 1/16, so \( 4N/16 = N/4 \)) and Y has had 2 (factor 1/4, so \( N/4 \)).

FThe decay constant and the exponential law extension

Definition

The decay constant \( k \) of a nuclide is the instantaneous probability per unit time that one undecayed nucleus decays; equivalently, it is the instantaneous fraction of undecayed nuclei that decay per unit time. Unit: s-1 (or min-1, h-1, y-1). For a very short interval \( \Delta t \), the probability is approximately \( k\Delta t \). Over a whole second, if \( k = 0.2 \) s-1, the exact probability is \( 1-e^{-0.2}=0.181 \), not exactly \( 0.2 \).

\[ A = kN \]
\[ N = N_0 e^{-kt}, \qquad A = A_0 e^{-kt} \]
\[ k = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{T_{1/2}} \]
\[ t = \frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{N_0}{N} = \frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{A_0}{A} \]
  • \( k \) is not the activity. Activity is decays per second for the whole sample and falls with time; \( k \) is per nucleus and is constant for a given nuclide. Like the half-life, \( k \) identifies the nuclide and is unchanged by temperature, pressure, chemical state or the amount of sample.
  • Large \( k \) means short half-life: \( k T_{1/2} = \ln 2 \) always. Both describe the same thing.
  • The decay law follows from \( A = kN \): the rate of loss of \( N \) is proportional to \( N \), and the only function that does that is the exponential. \( (1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}} \) and \( e^{-kt} \) are the same curve written two ways.
  • On a graph of \( \ln A \) (or \( \ln N \)) against \( t \), the points lie on a straight line: \( \ln A = \ln A_0 - kt \). Slope \( = -k \), intercept \( = \ln A_0 \). A mixture of two nuclides gives a curve that is straight only at the ends (the short-lived one dominates early, the long-lived one late).
  • The probability that a given nucleus decays within time \( t \) is \( 1 - e^{-kt} \), not \( kt \) (that approximation only works when \( kt \ll 1 \)).
  • Units. Keep \( k \) and \( t \) in the same unit. Convert \( k \) to s-1 before using \( A = kN \) with \( A \) in Bq.
  • Counting nuclei from mass. \( N = \dfrac{m}{M} \times N_A \), where \( M \) is the molar mass in grams (numerically the mass number) and \( N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23} \) mol-1; or \( N = m / (\text{mass of one atom}) \). Watch the mass units: 1 μg \( = 10^{-6} \) g.
Worked example

The number of undecayed nuclei in a sample falls to one third of its initial value in 50 days. (a) Find the decay constant. (b) How long does it take to fall to one tenth? (c) How long does it take to fall by one third?

  1. (a) \( N/N_0 = 1/3 = e^{-k(50)} \). Taking ln: \( \ln 3 = 50k \), \( k = 1.099/50 = 0.0220 \) d-1.
  2. (b) \( 1/10 = e^{-kt} \), \( t = \ln 10 / k = 2.303/0.0220 = 105 \) d.
  3. (c) Falling by one third leaves \( N/N_0 = 2/3 \). \( t = \ln(3/2)/k = 0.405/0.0220 = 18.5 \) d.

"Decreases by one third" and "decreases to one third" are different questions. Read the stem twice.

Worked example

A 1 μg sample of plutonium-239 has activity 2300 Bq. Find (a) the number of Pu-239 nuclei, (b) the decay constant, (c) the half-life in years. (\( N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23} \) mol-1; 1 y \( = 3.15 \times 10^{7} \) s.)

  1. (a) \( N_0 = \dfrac{1 \times 10^{-6}}{239} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.52 \times 10^{15} \).
  2. (b) \( k = A/N = 2300 / (2.52 \times 10^{15}) = 9.13 \times 10^{-13} \) s-1.
  3. (c) \( T_{1/2} = \ln 2 / k = 7.59 \times 10^{11} \) s \( = 7.59 \times 10^{11} / (3.15 \times 10^{7}) = 24\,100 \) y.

Concept check

Which statement about the decay constant of a radionuclide is incorrect?

A describes the activity, which depends on how much sample there is and falls with time. B and C are two wordings of the definition of \( k \); D is true because decay is spontaneous.

Concept check

A radionuclide has decay constant \( 1.02 \times 10^{-5} \) s-1. The time for the number of undecayed nuclei to decrease by one third is about

Decreasing by one third leaves 2/3: \( t = \ln(3/2)/k = 0.405/(1.02 \times 10^{-5}) = 3.98 \times 10^{4} \) s, B. A is the time to fall to one third (\( \ln 3 / k \)); C is one half-life; D uses \( kt = 1/3 \), the linear approximation.

Concept check

For a certain source, a graph of \( \ln A \) against \( t \) is a straight line through \( (0, 6.0) \) and \( (4.0 \text{ h}, 2.0) \). Which of the following is correct?

\( \ln A = \ln A_0 - kt \). Slope \( = (2.0 - 6.0)/4.0 = -1.0 \) h-1, so \( k = 1.0 \) h-1 and \( T_{1/2} = 0.693/1.0 = 0.69 \) h: C. The intercept is \( \ln A_0 = 6.0 \), so \( A_0 = e^{6} \approx 400 \) Bq, not 6 Bq (D).

Concept check

A nuclide has decay constant \( 0.05 \) s-1. The probability that a particular undecayed nucleus decays within the next 10 s is

The probability of surviving 10 s is \( e^{-kt} = e^{-0.5} = 0.61 \) (C is the survival probability). So the probability of decaying is \( 1 - 0.61 = 0.39 \), B. A is \( kt \), which overestimates because the chance is applied to a shrinking population; D is the probability per second.

Write it out

A 0.040 mg sample of iron-60 (molar mass 59.9 g) has an activity of 3400 Bq. (a) Find the number of iron-60 nuclei. (b) Find the decay constant. (c) Find the half-life in seconds, and comment on why iron-60 is found in samples of lunar soil that formed millions of years ago. (\( N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23} \) mol-1.) (5 marks)

  1. (a) \( N = \dfrac{0.040 \times 10^{-3}}{59.9} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 4.02 \times 10^{17} \).
  2. (b) \( k = A/N = 3400 / (4.02 \times 10^{17}) = 8.46 \times 10^{-15} \) s-1.
  3. (c) \( T_{1/2} = \ln 2 / k = 0.693 / (8.46 \times 10^{-15}) = 8.2 \times 10^{13} \) s (about 2.6 million years).
  4. A half-life of millions of years means only a small fraction of any iron-60 present when the soil formed has decayed, so measurable amounts remain.

Mass conversion (1), \( N \) (1), \( k \) (1), \( T_{1/2} \) (1), comment linking long half-life to survival (1).
