26.3

Radiation safety

Why ionizing radiation harms living tissue, how the harm is measured (dose, in sieverts, is not the same as activity, in becquerels), what typical doses look like, and the handling rules that keep dose low. Most exam marks here are for giving a reason with each precaution.

AWhy ionizing radiation is dangerous

Mechanism

α, β, γ and X-rays carry enough energy to knock electrons out of atoms. In living tissue this breaks up molecules in cells, in particular DNA. A cell may die, or its damaged DNA may make it divide uncontrollably, which is cancer. Damage to reproductive cells can be passed to offspring. The effects are cumulative: many small doses add up.

Source outside the bodySource inside the body (breathed in, swallowed, through a wound)
αLittle risk: stopped by a few centimetres of air or by the dead outer layer of skin.Most dangerous: all its energy is dumped within a fraction of a millimetre of tissue, giving intense ionization at that spot.
βSome risk: penetrates about a centimetre into tissue, so can burn skin and damage the eye.Dangerous: ionizes the tissue around where it collects, less intensely than α but over a few millimetres.
γMost dangerous of the three: passes through the body and can irradiate every organ from a distance.Still irradiates the whole body, but most of the energy passes out again, so the local damage is less than α.
  • Radioactive material gets inside the body as dust on food or fingers, as contaminated food and drink, and as radon, a radioactive gas.
  • Radon-222 is a member of the uranium-238 decay series and seeps out of rocks, soil and concrete. Indoors it accumulates, especially in poorly ventilated rooms and lower floors of buildings. Breathed in, it and its α-emitting daughters lodge in the lungs, where the α ionizes lung tissue directly: hence lung cancer in uranium miners. Radon is the largest single contributor to natural background dose (about 1.5 mSv per year). The remedy is ventilation.
  • "Hot springs rich in radon cure disease" is not supported by the physics: α from radon in the body ionizes healthy cells at random, which is damage, not treatment. Radiotherapy works only because the source is targeted at a tumour.
  • Higher activity does not automatically mean more harm. The harm depends also on the type of radiation, whether the source is inside or outside the body, how far away it is, how long the exposure lasts, and what shielding is between.

Concept check

A source emits both α and γ radiation. Which radiation is the greater hazard (i) if the source is swallowed, (ii) if the source is held against the skin?

Inside the body the strongly ionizing α, with all its energy absorbed locally, is the greater hazard. On the skin the α is stopped by the dead outer layer and does nothing, while the γ passes into the body. B. C reverses the logic; A and D ignore the location.

Concept check

Sample X has a higher activity than sample Y. Does X necessarily cause more biological harm to a person nearby?

B. Activity is what the source emits; harm depends on what the body absorbs and how. A weak α source swallowed can do more harm than a stronger γ source across the room. D still ignores distance, time and shielding; C is irrelevant to harm.

Concept check

Radon gas is a greater hazard to the lungs than to any other organ because

C. Radon is an α emitter; α is harmless outside the body and most harmful inside. Being a gas gets it inside, to the lungs specifically. D is true of radon but explains where it collects in a room, not why it harms the lungs. B is false (Rn-222 half-life is 3.8 days); A has the wrong radiation.

BActivity is emitted; dose is absorbed

Definitions

Absorbed dose \(D\) is the radiation energy absorbed per kilogram of tissue. It increases with the strength of the source, the time of exposure and closeness to the source.

Equivalent dose \(H\) weights that absorbed dose for the radiation type: α causes denser ionization than β, γ or X-rays. Effective dose \(E\) then weights the equivalent dose for the sensitivity of the exposed tissue, so it can represent the overall risk from an exposure:

\[ H = D \times W_{\text{radiation}}, \qquad E = H \times W_{\text{tissue}} \]

For exposure involving several tissues, add the appropriately weighted contributions to obtain \(E\). Equivalent and effective dose are measured in sievert (Sv); one sievert is large, so everyday values are quoted in millisieverts (mSv).

Radiation weighting factor \( W_{\text{radiation}} \)Tissue weighting factor \( W_{\text{tissue}} \) (examples)

Concept check

Which unit is used to measure the radiation equivalent dose received by a patient undergoing radiotherapy?

A. Becquerel measures the activity of the source; joule measures energy without reference to mass, radiation type or tissue; W m-2 is an intensity. Only the sievert folds in the biological weighting.

Concept check

Background in Hong Kong is about 2.4 mSv per year. A chest X-ray gives about 0.02 mSv and smoking 10 cigarettes a day gives about 18 mSv per year. In one year Sam smokes 5 cigarettes a day and has three chest X-rays. His total effective dose for the year is about

Smoking: half of 18 is 9 mSv. X-rays: \( 3 \times 0.02 = 0.06 \) mSv. Background: 2.4 mSv. Total \( 2.4 + 9 + 0.06 = 11.46 \approx 11.5 \) mSv, C. B is his dose above background; D uses 10 cigarettes a day; A forgets the smoking. Note that the cigarettes contribute far more than the X-rays.

Concept check

Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. The biological effect of radiation depends only on the energy absorbed, not on the type of radiation or the organ.
  2. For the same tissue, an equivalent dose of 1 Sv from an α source and 1 Sv from a γ source cause about the same biological effect.
  3. A dose of 1 Sv received in a short time is harmless.

(1) is false: that is why the weighting factors exist. (2) is true: the weighting for α (20) has already been applied in arriving at 1 Sv, so equal sieverts mean equal effect. (3) is false: 1 Sv at once causes radiation sickness. Answer B.

CKeeping the dose low

PrincipleWhy it works
Time: keep exposure shortDose accumulates with time. Plan the work so that sources are out of their containers for as little time as possible.
Distance: stay far awayRadiation spreads out in all directions from a small source, so intensity falls off rapidly with distance (roughly as \( 1/r^{2} \) for γ). α does not even reach you beyond a few centimetres.
Shielding: put dense material betweenLead or thick concrete stops β and greatly weakens γ. Wood or a thin aluminium box will not do for a γ source; a dense material is needed.
PrecautionReason
Store sources in a labelled lead-lined container, locked away, and return them immediately after useShielding, and minimum time out of the container
Handle with long forceps or tongs; never touch a source; never point it at anyoneDistance; keeps the source away from fingers and eyes and the beam away from people
Wear gloves, protective clothing and a lead apron where appropriate; wash hands after; no eating or drinking in the laboratoryPrevents radioactive material entering the body, where α and β do most harm
Wear a film badge or dosimeterRecords the dose received so exposure can be monitored and workers rotated. It does not reduce the dose
Only trained, authorised persons handle sources; use remote handling (robot arms, thick windows) for strong sourcesTime and distance for the people who face the largest doses
Label and store radioactive waste separately (for example contaminated syringes after a tracer test)Prevents contamination of others and of ordinary refuse
For patients: keep X-ray and CT examinations to those that are needed; shield parts of the body not being imagedMedical procedures are the main artificial contribution to dose
Worked example

Polonium-210, an α emitter with a half-life of 138 days, was used in a notorious poisoning. (a) Explain why a Po-210 source is far more dangerous swallowed than carried in a sealed container in a pocket. (b) Find its decay constant in s-1. (c) After how long would the activity in a contaminated object have fallen to a quarter of its initial value? (d) Why could traces still be detected weeks afterwards?

  1. (a) α is stopped by the container wall (or by skin), so outside the body the dose is negligible. Swallowed, the α deposits all its energy in the surrounding tissue, giving a very high, heavily weighted (\( W = 20 \)) dose.
  2. (b) \( k = \ln 2 / T_{1/2} = 0.693 / (138 \times 24 \times 3600) = 5.81 \times 10^{-8} \) s-1.
  3. (c) A quarter is two half-lives: \( 2 \times 138 = 276 \) d.
  4. (d) A 138-day half-life is long compared with a few weeks, so most of the Po-210 was still undecayed and still emitting.

Concept check

When performing an experiment with a radioactive source, can the radiation dose received be reduced by

  1. planning the experiment so that the handling time is shorter?
  2. storing the source in a lead container when it is not in use?
  3. wearing a film badge?

(1) is the time principle and (2) is shielding. A film badge (3) only records the dose already received; it does nothing to reduce it. Answer B.

Concept check

A school stores a γ source in a box. Which box material gives the best protection, and why?

C. γ is only weakened, never fully stopped; the thicker and denser the absorber, the more it is weakened, and lead is the practical choice. B is true only for α and β. A confuses electrical with radiation shielding; D is false: γ passes through wood or aluminium almost unchanged.

Write it out

A technician has to move a small γ source from its store to a demonstration bench and back. State four precautions the technician should take and give the reason for each. (4 marks)

  1. Carry the source in its lead container and take it out only at the bench: shielding, and minimum time exposed.
  2. Handle it with long tongs and keep it pointed away from people: distance, and no one in the beam.
  3. Plan the demonstration beforehand so the source is out for the shortest possible time: dose accumulates with time.
  4. Wear a film badge (and gloves; wash hands afterwards): so that the dose received is recorded, and so that no radioactive material is carried to the mouth.

Any four precaution-with-reason pairs. A precaution without its reason scores half.
