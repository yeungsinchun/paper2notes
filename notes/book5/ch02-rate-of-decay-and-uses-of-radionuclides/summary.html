4Traps that cost marks

Decayed nuclei are still there

They have become the daughter. Mass does not vanish; the sample becomes a parent-daughter mixture. "Amount decayed" is \( N_0 - N \), not \( N \).

Subtract background, then halve, then add it back

Half-life is read from the corrected count rate. On an uncorrected graph, read the time at (corrected/2 + background).

Long half-life is not weak

\( A = kN \): a large sample of a long-lived nuclide can out-emit a small sample of a short-lived one. Compare activities, not half-lives.

Half the time is not half the loss

After half a half-life 71% remains, not 75%. Decay is exponential; use \( (1/2)^{t/T} \) or \( e^{-kt} \) for any \( t \), including non-integers.

"By" one third is not "to" one third

Decreasing by one third leaves 2/3. Decreasing to one third leaves 1/3. The times differ by a factor of about 2.7.

k is not the activity

\( k \) is the probability of decay per nucleus per second, constant for the nuclide. \( A \) is the whole sample's decays per second and falls with time.

Half-life reasons come in pairs

Long enough to do the job; short enough not to linger (tracers) or long enough to stay steady (gauges, detectors, therapy machines). Give both halves.

Irradiated is not radioactive

Food, instruments and X-rayed patients absorb energy, not radioactive atoms. Only contamination with radioactive material makes something radioactive.

Activity falls, penetrating power does not

An old source emits fewer particles per second; each particle is as penetrating as ever. A film badge records dose; it does not reduce it.

5Last checks

Concept check

Source X falls from 100 Bq to 50 Bq in 60 s. Source Y falls from 100 Bq to 25 Bq in 30 s. The ratio of half-lives \( T_X : T_Y \) is

\( T_X = 60 \) s directly. For Y, 100 to 25 is two half-lives in 30 s, so \( T_Y = 15 \) s. Ratio 4 : 1, D. C is the trap of treating 100 to 25 as one half-life.

Concept check

Match each application to the property of the radionuclide it mainly relies on: thickness gauge, smoke detector, carbon-14 dating.

A gauge relies on the radiation being partly absorbed (penetrating power). A smoke detector relies on α ionizing the air. Dating relies on the half-life never changing. A. B swaps the first two; D forgets that the gauge count is about penetration.

Concept check

A sample contains two radionuclides P (half-life 1 h) and Q (half-life 2 h) and has total activity \( 6.0 \times 10^{6} \) Bq. Four hours later the total activity is \( 6.0 \times 10^{5} \) Bq. The initial activity of P was

In 4 h, P has 4 half-lives (factor 1/16) and Q has 2 (factor 1/4). With \( A_Q = 6.0 \times 10^{6} - A_P \): \( A_P/16 + (6.0 \times 10^{6} - A_P)/4 = 6.0 \times 10^{5} \). Multiply by 16: \( A_P + 4(6.0 \times 10^{6}) - 4A_P = 9.6 \times 10^{6} \), so \( 3A_P = 1.44 \times 10^{7} \), \( A_P = 4.8 \times 10^{6} \) Bq: C. A is the answer for Q.

Concept check

Two samples P and Q of different radionuclides are found to have the same corrected count rate at \( t = 10 \) min. P's count rate falls more steeply than Q's. Which of the following is correct at \( t = 10 \) min?

Steeper fall means a larger fraction decays per minute: larger \( k \), shorter half-life. Equal activities with \( A = kN \) means the sample with larger \( k \) has smaller \( N \): B. A is the common error of equating count rate with amount.
