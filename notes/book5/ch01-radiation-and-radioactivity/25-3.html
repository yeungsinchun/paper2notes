Section 25.3

Properties and detection of nuclear radiation

How α, β and γ ionize, how each detector turns that ionization into something visible, how far each radiation travels and what stops it, how to identify an unknown source from absorber readings, and how electric and magnetic fields separate the three.

Learning objectives

Students should be able to:

  • compare α, β and γ radiations in terms of their penetrating power, ranges, ionizing power, behaviour in electric field and magnetic field, and cloud chamber tracks

  • detect radiation with a photographic film and GM counter

  • detect radiation in terms of count rate using a GM counter

AIonizing power

NatureSpeedRelative massChargeIon-pairs per cm of airIonizing power
αhelium nucleusup to about 0.05\( c \) (some sources say 0.1\( c \))4 (≈ 7200 × electron)+220 000 to 50 000strongest
βfast electronup to 0.98\( c \)1/1840−150 to 500weaker

  • Why α ionizes most. It is slow, massive and carries two units of charge, so it spends a long time near each atom it passes and pulls strongly on the electrons. β is light, fast and singly charged; γ has no charge at all and ionizes only when it happens to be absorbed by an electron.
  • All three can ionize, γ included; the differences are in how many pairs per centimetre. Because it loses energy at every ionization, the strongest ionizer runs out of energy first: ionizing power and range go opposite ways (Section F).
  • Nothing is ionized in a vacuum: there are no atoms to ionize. Ionizing radiation is not only nuclear radiation; X-rays ionize too.

BIonized air conducts

Air is normally an insulator. Ionizing radiation gives it free charge carriers, so it conducts. Everything in this section follows from that single fact.

Change madeEffect on the current, and why
Replace the α source by a β source of similar activityCurrent falls: β makes far fewer ion-pairs per centimetre.
Move the α source slowly away from the platesCurrent falls to zero within a few centimetres: α has a range of only about 5 cm in air, so beyond that no air between the plates is ionized.
Pump the air out of the space between the platesCurrent falls to zero. There is nothing to ionize, so there are no charge carriers, even though the α now travels further.
Blow smoke between the platesCurrent falls: smoke particles capture the ions, making heavy, slow carriers. This is how an ionization smoke detector triggers its alarm (Chapter 26).
Bring the source near a charged metal sphere or a charged electroscopeThe sphere discharges and the leaf falls, whatever the sign of its charge: ions of the opposite sign are drawn to it and neutralise it.
Exam tip

It is the ions in the air that neutralise a charged object or carry the current, not the α particles themselves. A common wrong claim is that the α particles are attracted to the charged dust and neutralize it.

CCloud chamber tracks

How the chamber works

Dry ice cools the base of the chamber. A felt ring soaked in alcohol near the top fills the chamber with alcohol vapour, which becomes supersaturated in the cold layer near the base. Radiation passing through ionizes the air along its path, and the vapour condenses on the ions into droplets. Lit from the side, the line of droplets shows up as a white track. Because tracks appear at random places and at random moments, the chamber is also direct evidence that decay is random.

AppearanceWhy
αThick, straight, short (a few cm) and all about the same lengthThick because it is a strong ionizer (many droplets per cm). Straight because it is massive and is not knocked off course by collisions with air molecules. Short because it spends its energy quickly. Equal lengths because the α particles from one source are emitted with the same energy.
βThin, twisted, longerThin because it ionizes weakly. Twisted because it is light and is deflected in every collision.
γScattered, faint, short wisps, hardly observableγ itself leaves almost no ions; the few visible tracks are made by electrons it knocks out of atoms.
  • The cloud chamber is the only common detector that can tell the three radiations apart on its own. Film and a GM counter show that radiation is present and how much; they cannot say which kind without absorbers or a field.
  • Background radiation also makes tracks: a chamber with no source still shows occasional tracks.
  • In a magnetic field the tracks curve; the direction of curvature gives the sign of the charge and the radius gives the momentum.
  • In a chamber filled with helium, an α track sometimes forks at a right angle: the α made an oblique elastic collision with a helium nucleus of equal mass, and equal masses separate at 90°. This is evidence that the α particle is a helium nucleus.

Concept check

α tracks in a cloud chamber are thick and straight. Which of the following properties of α particles explain this?

  1. They are heavy.
  2. They have strong ionizing power.
  3. They have weak penetrating power.

Thick comes from (2): many ion-pairs per cm means many droplets. Straight comes from (1): a heavy particle is not deflected by the light air molecules it hits. (3) explains why the track is short, not why it is thick or straight, so it is not a reason for the property asked. Answer C.

Concept check

Which of the following detectors, used on its own, can distinguish α, β and γ radiation?

  1. photographic film
  2. cloud chamber
  3. GM counter

Only the cloud chamber shows a different track for each radiation. Film darkens and a GM tube counts whatever ionizing radiation reaches them; to identify the type you must add absorbers (Section G) or a field (Section H). Answer B.

DPhotographic film and the film badge

  • Film detects radiation because ionizing radiation blackens photographic film just as light does. A key or coin laid on film wrapped in black paper, with a β source above it, leaves a white shadow after developing: the radiation passed through the paper but not the metal. The paper envelope has been irradiated but is not radioactive.
  • Purpose of the badge. Worn by radiation workers to record the total (cumulative) dose received over weeks. Darker film means a stronger source or a longer exposure. It is cheap and simple but approximate, and it can only be read after the fact.
  • The wrapper keeps visible light out; otherwise the film would blacken anyway. The same wrapper stops all α particles, so a film badge can never detect α.
Radiation reaching the badgeDeveloped film

γ onlyAll three regions dark; lead region a little lighter than the others
β and γOpen window darkest (β + γ); aluminium region dark (γ); lead region lightest (part of the γ)
α onlyNo darkening anywhere: the wrapper stops α before it reaches the film

Write it out

A film badge has an open window, a 5 mm aluminium sheet and a 5 mm lead sheet in front of a wrapped film. Describe the appearance of the developed film if the wearer was exposed to (i) β only, (ii) γ only. Why is the film wrapped? (4 marks)

  1. (i) Only the region behind the open window is darkened; the aluminium and lead regions stay clear because 5 mm of aluminium (and lead) stops β.
  2. (ii) All three regions are darkened, the lead region least, because γ passes through aluminium almost unchanged and is only partly absorbed by lead.
  3. The wrapper stops visible light from blackening the film, so any darkening is due to the ionizing radiation alone.

Pattern of darkening (1 mark each case), the absorber reason (1), the wrapper reason (1).

EThe Geiger-Müller counter and count rate

How a GM counter works, step by step
  1. The tube contains argon at low pressure. A high voltage is kept between the central wire (positive, the anode) and the metal case (negative, the cathode). One end is a thin mica window.
  2. Radiation enters through the window and ionizes argon atoms, producing ion-pairs.
  3. The electrons are accelerated to the wire and the positive ions to the case; on the way they ionize more atoms, so a brief pulse of current flows through the tube.
  4. Each pulse is counted by the attached scaler (total count) or ratemeter (counts per second or per minute, the count rate).
\[ \text{corrected count rate} = \text{recorded count rate} - \text{background count rate} \]
  • Always measure the background first, with the source locked away, because every reading includes it. Decay is random, so readings fluctuate; take several readings and average them.
  • Background has a non-zero average count rate. A very short count can still be zero by chance, but repeated readings in Hong Kong typically average about 1 count per second (30 to 60 counts per minute).
  • Because it counts ion-pairs, the tube detects α and β more efficiently than γ, which usually passes straight through without ionizing anything. To detect α the plastic grid must be removed and the source placed within about 1.5 cm of the window; β and γ sources are placed about 5 cm away.
  • A GM counter is faster and more precise than film, but on its own it cannot tell which type of radiation it is counting.
  • The corrected count rate is proportional to the activity of the source (Chapter 26) but much smaller than it: the source radiates in all directions and the tube catches only the small fraction that enters its window, and some of that is absorbed on the way.

Concept check

With no radioactive source in the laboratory, a GM counter gives readings of 46, 58, 41, 55 and 50 counts per 100 s in five successive trials. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. The counter is faulty, because it should read zero without a source.
  2. The readings differ because radioactive decay is a random process.
  3. The best estimate of the background count rate is the mean, about 0.5 counts per second.

(1) is wrong: background radiation from radon, rocks, cosmic rays and the body reaches the tube everywhere, so a non-zero reading is normal. (2) and (3) are the two things to write about any set of background readings: the scatter is randomness, and averaging reduces its effect. Answer D.

FRange and penetrating power

Case to investigate
Absorbers in place 0 cpm

Show the diagnoses after you have tested the absorbers

Case 1: β + γ. Case 2: α + γ. Case 3: α + β + γ. In each case, compare one absorber result with the preceding result and subtract the background before deciding.

Definitions

The range of a radiation in a material is the distance it travels before it has lost all its energy and stops. Penetrating power is the ability to pass through material. A strong ionizer loses energy quickly, so strong ionizing power means short range and weak penetrating power: α < β < γ in penetrating power, the reverse of Table 25.4.

Range in airStopped byPenetrating power
αa few cm (about 5 cm)a sheet of paper, the skin, a few cm of airweakest
βa few m (about 5 m)about 5 mm of aluminium; the count falls gradually as the aluminium thickensstrong
γvery long (about 100 m, falling off with distance rather than stopping)never completely stopped: 25 mm of lead roughly halves the intensity, and each further 25 mm halves it againstrongest
Experiment: measuring the range of α in air
  1. With the source locked away, record the background count rate.
  2. Remove the plastic grid from the GM tube and line up the α source with the window, a few millimetres away. Record the count rate.
  3. Move the source away in small steps, recording the count rate each time, until the reading falls to the background value. Measure the separation with a metre rule: this is the range.
  4. On a graph of count rate against distance the curve drops and then levels off; the level part is background and the distance at which it is reached is the range. To rule out gravity as the reason the α "stop", repeat with the apparatus turned through 90°: the result is the same.
Worked example: γ through thick lead

A γ source gives 275 counts per minute at a GM tube; the background is 30 counts per minute. Estimate the reading with 100 mm of lead between source and tube, given that 25 mm of lead halves the γ intensity.

  1. Count from the source alone: \( 275 - 30 = 245 \) counts per minute.
  2. 100 mm is four thicknesses of 25 mm, so the source count is halved four times: \( 245 / 2^{4} \approx 15 \) counts per minute.
  3. Add the background back: reading \( \approx 15 + 30 = 45 \) counts per minute. It can never fall below 30.

Concept check

A GM tube is placed at increasing distances \( d \) from a source. The count rate falls steadily and from \( d = 4\ \mathrm{cm} \) onwards stays at about 28 counts per minute however far the tube is moved. Which conclusion is correct?

C. A reading that levels off and stays constant at large distance is the background (a source never reads below it). The radiation from the source stops arriving at 4 cm, a few centimetres, which is the range of α in air. β would reach several metres and γ would fall off only slowly, so A and B are out. D confuses the source with what reaches the detector: decay is not affected by where the tube is.

GIdentifying the radiation from absorber readings

The standard test: place the source in front of a GM tube and insert, in turn, a sheet of paper, about 5 mm of aluminium and about 25 mm of lead. Each absorber stops one more type. Compare each reading with the one before it and with the background.

How to write the answer: observation, reasoning, conclusion

For Example 25.6, one line per absorber:

  1. Paper: no significant change (700 → 700). Paper stops α. So the source emits no α.
  2. 5 mm aluminium: large drop (700 → 315). Aluminium stops β (and would stop α). So the source emits β.
  3. 25 mm lead: further drop (315 → 190) but still well above background (61). Lead only partly absorbs γ. So the source emits γ.
  4. Conclusion: β and γ, no α.
The hard cases

No paper step. 5 mm Al stops both α and β, so a drop at aluminium without a paper reading means "α or β or both", not "β". Lead only. A drop from air to 5 mm Pb proves nothing about which of α or β is present; a further drop from 5 mm to 15 mm Pb proves γ, and the background must be below the final reading. Background not given. If the reading keeps roughly halving as the lead thickens (say 90 → 46 from 25 to 50 mm), it has not reached a flat floor, so γ is still being absorbed and is present. Proving "X only". You need no significant drop at the other steps and a final reading at background. Repeated readings in a table (e.g. 210, 227; 224, 215) differ by randomness; a change only counts if it is well outside that scatter.

Concept check

Count rates (per second) measured with absorbers between a source and a GM tube: none 400; paper 398; 5 mm Al 180; 25 mm Pb 90; 50 mm Pb 46. The background was not measured. The source emits

Paper: no drop, so no α (D out). Aluminium: a large drop, so β is present (B out). Lead: the count halves again between 25 mm and 50 mm, so it is not sitting at a flat background floor; something is still being attenuated, which must be γ (A out). Answer C. This is how to reason when the background is not given: a reading that keeps changing with thickness is not background.

Concept check

With a source in front of a GM tube the readings are: no absorber 504; 5 mm lead 261; 15 mm lead 157 (counts per minute). No other absorbers were used. Which of the following can be concluded?

  1. The source emits β.
  2. The source emits γ.
  3. The background count rate is about 157 counts per minute.

Only (2). The drop from 261 to 157 as the lead thickens shows γ is present. The first drop (504 → 261) could be α, β or both being stopped by the 5 mm lead, so (1) is not proved. (3) is wrong because 15 mm of lead does not stop γ; the background is somewhere below 157. Answer B.

Write it out

A source is placed 10 cm from a GM tube. Two readings are taken with each absorber (counts per minute): no absorber 210, 227; paper 224, 215; 5 mm Al 45, 47; 10 mm Pb 48, 42. The background is about 45 counts per minute. Show that the source emits β only. (6 marks)

  1. Paper. Observation: no significant change (about 220 both times; the two repeat readings differ by about as much). Reasoning: paper stops α. Conclusion: no α is emitted.
  2. 5 mm aluminium. Observation: large drop to about 46. Reasoning: aluminium stops β. Conclusion: β is emitted.
  3. 10 mm lead. Observation: no further drop; the reading (about 45) equals the background. Reasoning: lead would reduce γ but there is nothing above background left to reduce. Conclusion: no γ is emitted.

Each absorber earns an observation mark and a reasoning-plus-conclusion mark. The "β only" claim needs all three lines: what paper did not do, what aluminium did, and that lead found nothing left.

HElectric and magnetic fields separate the three

Electric fieldMagnetic field

β (−1, light)Force against \( E \), towards the positive plate; direction fixed by negative chargeFleming's left-hand rule with the current opposite to the motion (negative charge); opposite side to α
γ (no charge)No force, no deflection
Exam tip

If a question says radiation from a radioactive source passes undeflected through a field, it is γ, never X-rays - a radioactive source does not emit X-rays, because X-rays are not nuclear radiation.

  • How to compare bending. The direction comes from the sign of charge. The amount depends on mass, charge magnitude, field strength and speed. At the same speed, a β has much greater acceleration because its mass is about 1/7200 of an α's, despite the α feeling twice the electric force. A question that gives equal kinetic energies instead needs the full comparison - mass alone does not decide it.
  • The collimator (two lead blocks with a narrow slit) turns the radiation leaving the source in all directions into a fine beam travelling in one direction, so that a deflection can be seen. If film is the detector, the apparatus must be in the dark.
  • β spreads out. β particles leave a source with a range of speeds, so they are deflected by different amounts and fan out over a region instead of hitting one spot. A GM tube at one point therefore catches only some of them, and the total β count with the field on is less than with it off. α particles from one source all have nearly the same speed, so they stay together.
  • In air the α is absorbed within a few centimetres and is usually missing from the picture; to see the α path the apparatus must be evacuated.
  • Reading a detector scan. Place γ at the undeflected position, β on the side the rule predicts, α (if any) on the other side. Two positions with equal small counts are both reading background. A missing peak does not prove a radiation is absent if it was deflected off the line of the scan or absorbed before arriving.

Concept check

A collimated beam from a source enters a uniform magnetic field directed into the page. Beyond the field a GM tube on the original line of the beam reads 95 counts per minute; a second GM tube placed below the line reads 520 counts per minute. Which deduction must be correct?

B. For a beam moving to the right in a field into the page, Fleming's rule sends a positive charge upwards and a negative charge downwards; a large count below the line must be β. A: 95 on the line could be γ, or could be entirely background, so γ is not proved. C: no tube was placed above the line, and α would in any case be absorbed by the air before arriving, so nothing can be said about α. D: 95 is an upper limit on the background (it may include some γ), not its value.

Concept check

A GM tube on the straight-through line from a source reads 184 counts per minute with no field and 82 counts per minute when a strong magnetic field is switched on across the beam. A tube far from the source reads 42 counts per minute. Estimate the β and γ count rates reaching the tube on the line, assuming any α is absorbed by the air.

The field removes the β from the line and leaves the γ and background: β ≈ 184 − 82 = 102. The far tube reads background only, so γ ≈ 82 − 42 = 40. Answer A. The other options forget to subtract either the field-on reading or the background.
