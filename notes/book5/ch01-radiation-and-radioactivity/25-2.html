Definitions

Protons and neutrons are together called nucleons. The atomic number \( Z \) is the number of protons in the nucleus. The mass number \( A \) is the total number of nucleons, so the number of neutrons is \( N = A - Z \). A neutral atom has \( Z \) electrons, equal to its number of protons.

  • Almost all the mass is in the nucleus (nucleons are about 1840 times heavier than electrons) but almost all the volume is empty space: the atom is about \( 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m} \) across, the nucleus \( 10^{-15} \) to \( 10^{-14}\ \mathrm{m} \). A nucleus in an atom is like a grain of sand in a football stadium.
  • The element is fixed by \( Z \) alone: every nucleus with 6 protons is carbon, whatever its number of neutrons.

Concept check

(a) The volume of an atom is mostly empty space.

The nucleus is about \( 10^{4} \) times smaller across than the atom, so it occupies about \( 10^{-12} \) of the atom's volume; the electrons are point-like. Almost everything in between is empty.

(b) Because the nucleus is so tiny, it holds only a small proportion of the atom's mass.

Size and mass are different things. One nucleon has about 1840 times the mass of an electron, so the nucleus holds more than 99.9% of the atom's mass in a tiny volume; it is extremely dense.

BNuclide notation and isotopes

¹H protium N = 0 · Z = 1

Definitions

A nuclide is a species of nucleus with a definite \( Z \) and \( A \), written \( {}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \): mass number on top, atomic number below, e.g. \( {}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C} \) or \( {}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U} \). Isotopes are nuclides of the same element (same \( Z \), same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons (different \( A \)).

  • Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties (same electrons) and slightly different physical properties (different mass). Natural carbon is about 99% \( {}^{12}\mathrm{C} \), 1% \( {}^{13}\mathrm{C} \) and a trace of \( {}^{14}\mathrm{C} \); only \( {}^{14}\mathrm{C} \) is radioactive. \( {}^{2}\mathrm{H} \) is called deuterium and \( {}^{3}\mathrm{H} \) tritium.
  • Some isotopes of every element are unstable. Nuclei with \( Z > 83 \) (heavier than bismuth) are all unstable: the strong nuclear force that binds nucleons acts only over a very short range, and in a large nucleus the electrical repulsion between the many protons wins.
Worked example

Three nuclides are \( {}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{P} \), \( {}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{Q} \) and \( {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{R} \). (a) How many elements are present? (b) Which nuclide has the fewest neutrons? (c) Which neutral atoms have the fewest electrons?

  1. (a) The element is fixed by \( Z \). P and Q both have \( Z = 6 \) (isotopes of one element); R has \( Z = 7 \). Two elements.
  2. (b) \( N = A - Z \): P has 8, Q has 6, R has 7. Q has the fewest neutrons.
  3. (c) Electrons in a neutral atom equal \( Z \): P and Q have 6 each, R has 7. P and Q.

Concept check

Which graph best shows how the number of neutrons \( N \) in the nucleus varies with the mass number \( A \) for the isotopes of one element of atomic number \( Z \)?

For isotopes \( Z \) is fixed, so \( N = A - Z \): a straight line of slope 1 that meets the \( A \)-axis at \( A = Z \) (a nucleus with no neutrons). D. A passes through the origin, which would mean \( N = A \), forgetting the protons. B has \( N \) falling as \( A \) rises, impossible when each extra nucleon is a neutron. C keeps \( N \) constant while \( A \) changes, which would require \( Z \) to change, i.e. a different element.

Concept check

Consider the nuclides \( {}^{40}_{18}\mathrm{Ar} \), \( {}^{40}_{19}\mathrm{K} \), \( {}^{40}_{20}\mathrm{Ca} \) and \( {}^{42}_{20}\mathrm{Ca} \). Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. \( {}^{40}\mathrm{Ar} \), \( {}^{40}\mathrm{K} \) and \( {}^{40}\mathrm{Ca} \) are isotopes because they have the same mass number.
  2. \( {}^{40}\mathrm{Ca} \) and \( {}^{42}\mathrm{Ca} \) are isotopes.
  3. \( {}^{40}\mathrm{Ar} \) and \( {}^{42}\mathrm{Ca} \) have the same number of neutrons.

Isotopes share \( Z \), not \( A \). (1) is the classic trap: three different elements happen to have \( A = 40 \); they are not isotopes. (2) is correct: same \( Z = 20 \), different \( A \). (3): \( N = 40 - 18 = 22 \) for argon-40 and \( 42 - 20 = 22 \) for calcium-42, so it is also correct. Answer C.

CThree ways an unstable nucleus sheds energy

Vocabulary

An unstable nuclide (a radionuclide or radioisotope) gets rid of excess energy by emitting radiation; this is radioactive decay. The original nucleus is the parent, the nucleus left behind is the daughter. When the daughter is a different element (different \( Z \)) the change is a transmutation. Decay is spontaneous and random: nothing outside the nucleus (heat, pressure, chemical bonding) starts, stops, speeds or slows it.

  • Conservation. In every equation the total mass number and the total atomic number (charge) are the same on both sides. This is how you balance an unknown.
  • Consequences. Any α or β decay produces a different element, so it is always a transmutation. γ emission changes neither \( A \) nor \( Z \); the nuclide only loses energy. That is why a source such as Ra-226 or Am-241 is listed as emitting "α and γ": the γ follows the α from the excited daughter.
  • Answer format. If asked "state the kind of radiation", write "α particle" or "β particle", not "α decay". In β decay the particle emitted is an electron; no neutron is emitted.
Worked example

Write the equation for (a) the α decay of radium-226 (\( Z = 88 \)) to radon, and (b) the β decay of carbon-14 (\( Z = 6 \)) to nitrogen.

  1. (a) Put the parent on the left, the daughter and the particle on the right; balance top and bottom: \( 226 = A + 4 \) gives \( A = 222 \); \( 88 = Z + 2 \) gives \( Z = 86 \).
    \[ {}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} \]
  2. (b) The electron has \( A = 0 \), \( Z = -1 \): \( 14 = A + 0 \) gives \( A = 14 \); \( 6 = Z + (-1) \) gives \( Z = 7 \).
    \[ {}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{0}_{-1}e \]

Concept check

Which equation correctly represents the β decay of lead-214, \( {}^{214}_{82}\mathrm{Pb} \)?

B: the electron is emitted (right-hand side) and the totals balance: \( 214 = 214 + 0 \), \( 82 = 83 + (-1) \). A puts the electron on the left as if it were absorbed. C lowers \( A \), but an electron has zero mass number. D lowers \( Z \), forgetting that the electron's charge is −1, so the daughter must have one more proton.

Concept check

A nuclide R undergoes β decay, and the daughter then emits a γ ray. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. After the β decay the nucleus has one more proton and one fewer neutron.
  2. The β particle is one of the orbital electrons of the atom R.
  3. The γ emission leaves the mass number and atomic number unchanged.

(1) is what β decay is: a neutron becomes a proton plus an electron, so \( Z \) rises by 1, \( N \) falls by 1 and \( A \) is unchanged. (3) is right: a γ ray carries away energy only. (2) is the common misconception; the electron is created inside the nucleus at the moment of decay. Answer C.

Write it out

Bismuth-213 (\( Z = 83 \)) undergoes β decay to polonium (Po), which then undergoes α decay to lead (Pb). Write the two decay equations. (HKDSE 2026 Paper 1B Q12, 2 marks)

\[ {}^{213}_{83}\mathrm{Bi} \rightarrow {}^{213}_{84}\mathrm{Po} + {}^{0}_{-1}e \]
\[ {}^{213}_{84}\mathrm{Po} \rightarrow {}^{209}_{82}\mathrm{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} \]

One mark for each equation with both numbers correct on every symbol. Check each line: top row 213 = 213 + 0 and 213 = 209 + 4; bottom row 83 = 84 − 1 and 84 = 82 + 2.

DDecay series

The daughter of a decay is often itself unstable and decays again. The chain continues until a stable nuclide is reached. Uranium-238 goes through 14 decays (8 α and 6 β) before ending at stable lead-206: \( {}^{238}\mathrm{U} \xrightarrow{\alpha} {}^{234}\mathrm{Th} \xrightarrow{\beta} {}^{234}\mathrm{Pa} \xrightarrow{\beta} {}^{234}\mathrm{U} \xrightarrow{\alpha} \cdots \rightarrow {}^{206}\mathrm{Pb} \). Members of a series with the same \( Z \) are isotopes of one another.

Worked example: counting α and β

Plutonium-239 decays through a series to lead-207. How many α and how many β decays are involved?

\[ {}^{239}_{94}\mathrm{Pu} \rightarrow {}^{207}_{82}\mathrm{Pb} \]
  1. Only α changes \( A \), by 4 each time: \(\Delta A = 239 - 207 = 32\) → nα = 32 / 4 = 8
  2. Those 8 α alone would take \( Z \) to \( 94 - 2 \times 8 = 78 \).
  3. Each β raises \( Z \) by 1 and the final \( Z \) is 82, so nβ = 82 − 78 = 4

Write it in exam form as two equations: \( 4n_{\alpha} = \Delta A \) and \( 2n_{\alpha} - n_{\beta} = \Delta Z \).

Exam tip: which nuclides can belong to a series?

Because only α changes \( A \), and by 4 each time, every member of a series has a mass number that differs from the parent's by a multiple of 4. A candidate with the wrong \( A \) can be ruled out immediately. Then check \( Z \): after \( n_{\alpha} \) alphas and \( n_{\beta} \) betas, \( Z = Z_0 - 2n_{\alpha} + n_{\beta} \) with \( n_{\beta} \geq 0 \). Also: "state the end product of the Ra-226 series from Pb-206, Pb-207, Pb-208" is answered by \( 226 - 206 = 20 \), a multiple of 4.

Concept check

Neptunium-237, \( {}^{237}_{93}\mathrm{Np} \), decays through a series to stable bismuth-209, \( {}^{209}_{83}\mathrm{Bi} \). The numbers of α and β decays are

\( \Delta A = 237 - 209 = 28 = 4 \times 7 \), so 7 α. After them \( Z = 93 - 14 = 79 \); to reach 83 needs 4 β. B. A and D get the α count wrong (28 is not 24 or 32). C subtracts the \( Z \) values directly (\( 93 - 83 = 10 \), then confuses the bookkeeping) instead of first applying the α step.

Concept check

A uranium-238 nucleus \( {}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U} \) undergoes an α decay, then two β decays, then another α decay, to become nuclide X. What are the atomic number and mass number of X?

Two α: \( A \) falls by 8 to 230 and \( Z \) by 4 to 88. Two β: \( Z \) rises by 2 to 90, \( A \) unchanged. So (90, 230), A. B and D forget the second α in \( A \); C forgets that β raises \( Z \).

Concept check

A nuclide W decays as follows: W → X (α decay), X → Y (β decay), Y → Z (β decay). Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. X has one more proton than Y.
  2. W has two more neutrons than X.
  3. W and Z are isotopes.

Track \( (Z, N) \). α: \( (Z-2, N-2) \); each β: \( (Z+1, N-1) \). So X = \( (Z-2, N-2) \), Y = \( (Z-1, N-3) \), Z = \( (Z, N-4) \). (1) X has one fewer proton than Y, false. (2) true: W has two more neutrons than X. (3) true: W and Z have the same \( Z \) (and \( A \) differs by 4), so they are isotopes. Answer D.

Concept check

Radium-226, \( {}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra} \), decays by a series of α and β decays. Which of the following nuclides cannot be a member of its series?

\( A \) must fall in steps of 4 from 226. B has \( 226 - 216 = 10 \), not a multiple of 4, so it is impossible whatever the β count. The others pass: A is one α; C is 3 α (\( Z = 82 \)) then 1 β; D is 4 α (\( Z = 80 \)) then 2 β. (This is the reasoning behind HKDSE 2025 Paper 1A Q32.)

Concept check

A school radium-226 source is described as an α emitter, yet a detector near it also registers β and γ radiation. The best explanation is that

B. Ra-226 is part of the U-238 series; its daughters Rn-222, Po-218, Pb-214, Bi-214 and so on accumulate inside the sealed source and several of them are β or γ emitters. The examiners' report for this question singled out A as the wrong answer many candidates gave. C describes no real process, and D cannot explain readings far above background.

ERadioactive sources in the laboratory and how to handle them

  • Schools may use only weak sealed sources like this one. Radioactive material that is not enclosed (rock samples, radon gas) is an open source.
  • Radiation from the source cannot make the room, the bench or the student radioactive (see irradiation vs contamination in Section F). The danger is the dose of ionizing radiation absorbed while the source is out.

Inside vs outside the body

Outside the body γ is the most dangerous: it penetrates to internal organs, while α is stopped by a few centimetres of air or the dead outer layer of skin. Inside the body (breathed in or swallowed) α is the most dangerous: it is the strongest ionizer and dumps all its energy into the surrounding cells. This reversal is why radon gas is a hazard (Section F) and why α is never used as a medical tracer (Chapter 26).

Concept check

A teacher takes a sealed γ source out of its box for a demonstration. Which of the following precautions is/are justified by the reason given?

  1. Hold the source with long forceps, because the intensity received falls with distance from the source.
  2. Store the source in a thick wooden box, because wood absorbs γ rays well.
  3. Keep the demonstration short, because the dose absorbed increases with exposure time.

(1) and (3) are the two principles that appear in every marking scheme: distance and time. (2) fails on the reason: γ passes through wood almost unhindered; the box must be lead-lined. Answer C.

Concept check

A source emits α and γ radiation. Which type of radiation is the more dangerous (i) if the source is accidentally swallowed, and (ii) if the sealed source is held in the hand?

B. Inside the body nothing shields the tissue from the α, which is the strongest ionizer, so α does the most damage. In the hand the α is stopped by the metal case or by the skin and does nothing, while γ passes through the hand and into the body. A and D ignore the location; C reverses the logic.

FBackground radiation

A detector registers ionizing radiation even with no source in the room. This background radiation comes mostly from natural sources and reaches everyone, everywhere, all the time. In Hong Kong a GM counter typically reads about 1 count per second from background; the average annual dose is about 2.4 mSv, which is far too low to cause any apparent harm.

SourceWhat it is and what affects it
RadonA colourless, odourless radioactive gas produced in the U-238 decay series in rocks and soil. It seeps out of the ground and out of granite and concrete building materials and collects in poorly ventilated rooms. Being a gas it is inhaled, and it and its daughters emit α inside the lungs. The remedy is ventilation (open windows), not sealing the room or wearing a dust mask.
Rocks and soilRadioactive minerals (uranium, thorium, potassium-40) present since the Earth formed. Granite areas and caves have higher background than the open air above them.
Cosmic raysVery energetic particles from space. The atmosphere shields us, so the dose is higher on mountains and in aircraft (airline crews receive a measurably larger dose) and lower deep inside a cave or mine.
Food, drink, the bodyAll living things contain a little potassium-40 and carbon-14; so does sea water. Every human body is slightly radioactive.
ArtificialMedical X-rays and radiotherapy (by far the largest artificial share), industrial sources, consumer products such as smoke detectors, fallout from past nuclear weapon tests, and any leakage from nuclear power stations. Note that leakage counts as artificial background.
Irradiation vs contamination

An object that has been irradiated has only absorbed the energy of the radiation; it does not become radioactive. A patient after an X-ray, luggage after a security scan, or a wrapped film that has darkened under a source are irradiated, not radioactive. An object is contaminated when radioactive material itself gets onto or into it; it then goes on emitting radiation until the material is removed or decays. Washing hands after handling a source removes possible contamination.

Concept check

Which of the following may contain sources of ionizing radiation?

  1. sea water
  2. a rock sample
  3. the human body

All three, D. Radioactive isotopes of common elements (potassium-40, carbon-14, uranium and thorium traces) are present in rock, dissolved in sea water and inside our own tissues. Fewer than half of candidates chose this in 2021; the trap is to think "radioactive" means "unusual".

Concept check

With no source nearby, a GM counter reads about 30 counts per minute. A student then holds a mobile phone that is making a call next to the tube. Which statement is correct?

B. A GM tube counts ionizing events; microwaves cannot ionize argon atoms, so the phone is invisible to it. The stated 30 counts per minute is already a normal non-zero average background reading; an individual very short count can still be zero by chance. C confuses "radiation" with "ionizing radiation". D has no mechanism.

Concept check

A GM counter shows a higher background count inside a cave than in the open air outside it. Which source is least likely to be responsible for the increase?

C. The rock above a cave shields it from cosmic rays, so the cosmic contribution is lower underground, not higher. Rock, soil and trapped radon all raise the count. The reverse question, "why is background higher on a mountain top?", has the answer cosmic rays: less atmosphere above to absorb them.

Concept check

(a) After a chest X-ray a patient is slightly radioactive for a few hours.

The patient was irradiated: the X-rays deposited energy and left. No radioactive material was put into the body, so nothing goes on emitting. Contamination requires the radioactive substance itself.

(b) The best way to reduce the radon level in a flat is to keep the windows closed so that no radon can enter from outside.

Radon comes from the building materials and the ground beneath the flat, and a closed room lets it accumulate. Ventilation dilutes it with outside air, where the level is low.

(c) Background radiation varies from place to place.

Granite regions, high altitude and poorly ventilated basements all give higher readings; the sea surface gives lower. This is why a background reading must be taken at the place and time of every experiment.
