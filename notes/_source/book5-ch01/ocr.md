Discovery of radioactivity

In 1896, Henri Becquerel (1852-1908) discovered radioactivity by accident.

One day, he planned to expose some uranium salt to sunlight in the hope
of getting X-rays, but that day was too cloudy and so he put the salt into a
drawer. Later, he found that the photographic plate underneath the salt was
blackened. He then realized that the uranium salt was emitting ionizing
radiation by itself.

Fig. a Henri Becquerel and his photographic plate

After Becquerel’s discovery, Pierre (1859-1906) and Marie (1867-1934)
Curie became interested in the new field of radioactivity, a term coined by
them. Together they discovered two radioactive elements, polonium
(named after Poland, Marie’s homeland) and radium (named after the Latin
word for ray).

Fig. b Marie and Pierre Curie

At the Curies’ time, no one knew the health risks posed by exposure to
ionizing radiation. Marie carried radioactive substances in her pocket and
stored them in desk drawers. She eventually died of a disease caused by
prolonged radiation exposure. After more than 100 years, her papers,
furniture and even cookbooks are still radioactive.
